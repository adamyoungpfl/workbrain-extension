## Slide 10 — 09 · The measured group effect

**Individually faster. Collectively identical.**

Each person's output improves, and the group's diversity shrinks. Ten people on one team produce ten versions of the same thing — and none of them chose that.

## Slide 13 — 12 · What choices do they have right now?

**Sit in their seat for a second.**

Someone on your team opens the same tool everyone else has. Here is the whole menu available to them today:

- **Avoid it** — Stay away from it. Reasonable — they have heard it is unreliable, or that IT has not blessed it. Meanwhile the people they compete with are twice as fast, and nobody tells them that is what is happening.
- **Wing it** — Use it for everything, exactly as it comes. Also reasonable — it is right there and it is fast. Their work now sounds like everyone's work, and the expertise you hired them for never makes it onto the page.

## Slide 14 — 13 · The third option

**Teach them to author their own context.**

Each person writes a short, plain-text file about their own work — their names for things, their judgment, the standards they hold — and hands it to whatever tool they already use. Nothing routes through us. No account. Nothing transmitted.

## Slide 15 — 14 · What they walk out with

**A skill that belongs to them.**

It works in a tool that doesn't exist yet, in a model nobody has released. The files belong to each person — and the written methods stay with the team.

- **Their files** — Plain markdown they own — who they are, how they work, what they repeat. On their device, not ours.
- **A method** — One job they do every week, written down well enough that a tool — or a new coworker — can follow it.
- **Proof** — Their own before-and-after, run with their own eyes, on their own work.
- **Institutional memory** — The way your best person actually does the job, written down — so when they are out, or gone, the method does not leave with them.

## Slide 16 — 15 · What you can stand behind

**Numbers, not testimonials.**

- **You can say what "good" means** — Four plain checks anyone can run in five minutes, published on a public page. Your most skeptical engineer can audit the claim without asking us anything.

## Slide 19 — Appendix A · Push back on us

- **"The homogenization research is early."** It is, and we say so on the slide. What isn't early: memory on by default, profiles nobody authored, and the before-and-after each person measures on their own work. The case never rests its weight on the scary slide.
- **"This sounds anti-AI. We just rolled it out company-wide."** Four 90-minute sessions of hands-on building with the tool each person already uses. The foundations hour exists so the adoption is informed. We teach use — the caution is the seatbelt, not an argument against the car.
- **"My team needs output now, not voice lessons."** Everyone's output is polished now, and buyers pattern-match the house style — in proposals, in pitches, in hiring. When polish is table stakes, what survives is whose judgment shows. That is a revenue argument, not a taste argument.

---

## Slide 20 — Appendix B · The honest concessions

- **"Isn't this prompt training with extra steps?"** Prompt phrasing doesn't transfer between tools — the research is clear. An owned file does. The room demonstrates the difference to itself, in five minutes, on their own work.
- **"You teach people not to sound like the machine… using the machine."** The same way a writing class uses a word processor. The tool drafts; the person and their file decide. Improve what they do — don't replace it.
- **What we concede without a fight** Average is fine for boilerplate; the file exists for the work that carries someone's name. And a context file steers voice — it does not guarantee it. The promise is authorship plus evidence, never magic.
