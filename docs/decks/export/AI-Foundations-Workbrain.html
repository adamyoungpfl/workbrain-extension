01 · What they're actually holding

Hope this email finds you  ___ 

You filled that in before I finished the sentence. So does the assistant your people were handed — for every word it writes. It is autocomplete at enormous scale: it continues with what most people would say.

A simplification an expert accepts: predicting the most likely next word is the literal training objective.

02 · The objective, plainly

It always bets on average.

Not a flaw — the definition. The most likely word is the most common one, so every draft your people are handed starts life as the most common version of the thought. They can beat that. Nobody has shown them how.

03 · The half nobody mentions

And it reads them the same way.

Interpretation is prediction too. Handed your coordinator's request, it hears the most likely meaning — the average asker's meaning. What it misreads is exactly what you value in her: her judgment, her shorthand, the things she knows that most people don't. And it never says so. It answers a question nobody asked, confidently.

Rewording alone swings measured accuracy up to 76 points (Sclar et al., ICLR 2024). Models misread — and covertly penalize — non-standard dialects (Hofmann et al., Nature 2024). It does not read all of your people equally well.

07 · Being fair to the machine

The honest ledger.

What that memory does well

Zero effort — it fills itself. Continuity across chats. The mundane, reliably: tone, recurring facts, updates nobody would think to record. Concede every bit of it; it's true.

What it cannot do

Confirm anything with the person. Tell them when it rewrote itself. Move with them to another assistant — or another employer. Read what is distinctive about them as anything but noise.

Let them use it — and don't let it be the only thing that speaks for them. It is the model's reading of your person, stored: each misread shapes the next. A memory nobody authored is a lens nobody can focus.

08 · The measured group effect

Individually faster.
Collectively identical.

The research finding on AI assistance: each person's output improves, and the group's diversity shrinks. Ten people produce ten versions of the same thing — and none of them chose that.

Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances 2024 · Padmakumar & He, ICLR 2024 · Shumailov et al., Nature 2024. A measured trajectory, still early — we say so out loud.

09 · What it costs them

The day their work stops sounding like them.

For the person: the contribution that used to be recognizably theirs stops getting noticed — and in a hiring pool where every application is polished by the same tool, sameness is the rejection. For you: the discount lands on your organization's name, not on the software.

10 · One question to keep

The difference has to come from your people.

Your competitor's proposal was written by the same model as yours. So was the grant application from the organization across town. Have your people been given any way to put themselves back into the work?

11 · The two default policies

Ban it

They use it anyway — personal accounts, at home, unmanaged and unsupported. You lose the leverage and the oversight, and nobody learns anything.

Let it run

Unsupported adoption converges everyone on the same house style. You keep the leverage and quietly spend what made your people distinct.

Both defaults teach your people nothing. There is a third option, and it is a teaching one.

12 · The third option

Teach them to author
their own context.

Each person writes a short, plain-text file about their own work — their names for things, their judgment, the standards they hold — and hands it to whatever assistant they already use. Nothing routes through us. No account. Nothing transmitted. The tool finally reads and writes from who they actually are.

Appendix A · Push back on us

"The homogenization research is early."

It is, and we say so on the slide. What isn't early: memory on by default, profiles nobody authored, and the before-and-after each of your people measures on their own work. The case never rests its weight on the scary slide.

"This sounds anti-AI. We're trying to drive adoption."

Four evenings of hands-on building with each person's own assistant. The foundations hour exists so the adoption is informed. We teach use — the caution is the seatbelt, not an argument against the car.

"My people need output now, not voice."

Everyone's output is polished now, and readers pattern-match the house style — in hiring, in sales, in grant review. When polish is table stakes, what survives is whose judgment shows. That is an output argument, not a taste argument.

Appendix B · The honest concessions

"Isn't this prompt training with extra steps?"

Prompt phrasing doesn't transfer between assistants — the research is clear. An owned file does. Your people demonstrate the difference themselves, in the room, in five minutes.

"You teach people not to sound like the machine… using the machine."

The same way a writing class uses a word processor. The tool drafts; the person and their file decide. Improve what they do — don't replace it.

What we concede without a fight

Average is fine for boilerplate; the file exists for the work that carries someone's name. And a context file steers voice — it does not guarantee it. The promise is authorship plus evidence, never magic.
