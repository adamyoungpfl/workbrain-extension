# The community cut — audience audit

Adam, 2026-09-11: "The goal is to go to a YMCA or Library or Goodwill or
Salvation Army or other Workforce Development services… to offer to their
members who are unemployed, underemployed or young and learning new
skills… I want to be sure that I am not confusing who is getting the
benefit, who is paying and why they would pay for these people to receive
this free training."

## The three roles, stated once so every slide can be checked against them

| Role | Who | What they get |
|---|---|---|
| **The decision maker** — the person in the room | A program director, branch manager, workforce-services lead. Not a buyer of software; a person who adds programs to a calendar and answers to a board or a funder. | A ready-made course they can offer, a credential their participants can show, reporting they can put in a board packet, and no ongoing cost or licence to defend. |
| **The participant** — the beneficiary | Their members: unemployed, underemployed, changing careers, young and building skills. Sometimes the org's own frontline staff. | Four sessions, files they own, a proof they ran, a certificate — free to them, permanently. |
| **The payer** | The organization, or a sponsor/grant it already holds. | The per-cohort cost, once. Nothing after. |

**The one sentence the deck must never blur:** the organization pays, the
participant benefits, and nothing is ever sold to the participant.

## The three fixes this audit produced (applied)

1. **Slide 12 was talking to a business.** For a library or a YMCA, the
   rival framing does not land — they do not lose deals. Rewritten to the
   participant's competition, which is real and immediate: *every
   application in the stack was written by the same model.* The
   "organization across town" line is gone from the community cut.
2. **"Employees" crept in where the room hears "members."** Swept: the
   community cut now says *members, participants, and the people you
   serve*, with "your staff" reserved for the moments the offer genuinely
   extends to the organization's own team.
3. **Who pays was implicit on slides 15–16 and explicit only on 17.**
   Slide 15 now closes with the line a director repeats upward: *they
   keep all of it, free, whether or not they ever come back to you.*

## Two things this deck still does NOT say, and probably should

Adam's calls, not applied:

1. **Where the money usually comes from.** Most of these organizations do
   not have $4,800 sitting in a program line; they have grant cycles,
   workforce-development funds (WIOA), United Way allocations, a local
   employer sponsor, or a foundation relationship. One sentence on the
   offer slide — *"most hosts fund this from an existing workforce or
   digital-literacy line, and we will write the paragraph for your
   application"* — would remove the largest silent objection in the room.
2. **What the host has to do.** A director's real fear is staff time.
   The deck says "a room with wifi and a repeating slot," which is true
   but easy to disbelieve. An explicit *"your staff attend if they want
   to; they do not run anything"* is worth its line.

## Where the two cuts must stay different

The employer cut may talk about deals, competitors, and institutional
memory. **The community cut may not** — its story is a member's
employability, dignity and voice. The divergence table in
`docs/AI-FOUNDATIONS.md` §7b is the contract; this audit is its
audience-side companion.
